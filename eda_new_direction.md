# EDA: Evidence for New Project Direction
**Focal Neighborhood: East Boston**

---

## Hypothesis
Landlords are motivated to displace existing tenants in order to re-let at higher rates.
As investor speculation increases, rents rise and evictions follow — not because tenants
break rules, but because they can no longer afford to stay.

---

## 1. Focal Neighborhood Selection

East Boston is the strongest candidate. It hits all three pillars simultaneously:

| Metric | 2015 | 2018 | 2022 | Change |
|--------|------|------|------|--------|
| Median est. rent | $1,708/mo | $2,417/mo | $3,008/mo | **+76%** |
| Corporate ownership rate | 6% | 11% | 22% | **+267%** |
| Owner-occupancy rate | 42% | 41% | 42% | flat |

Corporate ownership grew from **3% (2004) → 30% (2024)** — the second largest jump
of any Boston neighborhood, alongside South Boston Waterfront.

---

## 2. The Displacement Story: Before/After Affordability

> If a tenant's budget was the **2018 median ($2,417/mo)**, **71% of 2022 sales are
> now above that budget.**

A tenant with an unchanged budget from 2018 is locked out of 7 out of 10 units
available today in the same neighborhood. They didn't become poorer — the market
moved around them.

Rent growth timeline:
- 2015: $1,708/mo
- 2016: $2,004/mo (+17%)
- 2017: $2,146/mo (+7%)
- 2018: $2,417/mo (+13%)
- 2019: $2,854/mo (+18%) ← investor peak year (47.7% of purchases)
- 2020: $2,708/mo (slight dip, COVID)
- 2021: $2,879/mo (+6%)
- 2022: $3,008/mo (+4%)

---

## 3. Investor Activity Driving the Market

Investor purchases in East Boston by year:

| Year | Total Sales | Investor Purchases | Investor Share |
|------|------------|-------------------|----------------|
| 2015 | 382 | 151 | 39.5% |
| 2016 | 397 | 134 | 33.8% |
| 2017 | 465 | 165 | 35.5% |
| 2018 | 341 | 132 | 38.7% |
| 2019 | 348 | 166 | **47.7%** |
| 2020 | 303 | 120 | 39.6% |
| 2021 | 425 | 156 | 36.7% |
| 2022 | 434 | 156 | 35.9% |

2019 was the peak investor year (nearly 1 in 2 purchases). Rents surged most sharply
in 2019 (+18%), directly tracking the investor activity spike.

Investor breakdown by type (2015–2022):
- Small investors: 752
- Medium investors: 167
- Institutional: 158
- Large investors: 103

---

## 4. Evictions: People Are Being Priced Out

**Citywide case types:**
- Non-payment of rent: **65.1%**
- No cause: 16.7%
- Cause: 15.7%

People aren't being evicted for violating leases — they can't pay rent they
could once afford.

**East Boston specifically:**
- Total filings (2019–2022): 354
- Non-payment of rent: **61.9%**
- Filing volume spiked from 13 (2019) → 144 (2021), as corp ownership jumped
  from 12% → 21% in the same period.

---

## 5. Narrative Flow

This data supports a clean 5-beat story entirely within East Boston:

1. **Context** — "In 2004, 3% of East Boston was investor-owned." (choropleth)
2. **The shift** — "By 2024, that number is 30%." (time slider animation)
3. **Rising costs** — "Median estimated rent rose 76% from 2015 to 2022." (line chart)
4. **Displacement** — "A tenant with a 2018 budget can no longer afford 71% of
   today's units." (before/after dot plot)
5. **Evictions** — "61.9% of East Boston eviction filings are non-payment of rent —
   not rule-breaking, but priced out." (bar chart + scatter)

---

## 6. Alternative: South Boston Waterfront

If East Boston doesn't work for any reason, South Boston Waterfront had the
**largest corp ownership growth** (6% → 38%, +32pp) and is geographically
concentrated, making it easy to tell a tight story. However, it is a wealthier
area with less renter vulnerability, which weakens the displacement narrative.
East Boston is the stronger choice for audience connection.
